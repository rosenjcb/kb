\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Targets}

\textbf{Raylib (external benchmark).}  We target the
\href{https://github.com/raysan5/raylib}{raylib C library}---a mature,
well-documented game-programming framework---as the primary \emph{external}
benchmark: it is not the KB codebase (eliminating evaluator familiarity bias),
has rich cross-module structure for graph retrieval, and has stable upstream
documentation.

\textbf{KB self-check.}  Suite \texttt{kb} evaluates KB against its
own codebase---twelve architecture, workflow, and adversarial questions (including
fact-curator and out-of-scope probes).  This smoke test
stress-tests retrieval grounding on a repo whose structure the authors know well.

\textbf{Expanded portfolio.}  To stress parsers and retrieval across languages and
architectural paradigms, we add seven production-grade suites:
\texttt{nifi} (Java NAR classloading / dataflow),
\texttt{shellcheck} (Haskell monadic AST linting),
\texttt{lazygit} (Go TUI),
\texttt{duckdb} (C++ vectorized OLAP),
\texttt{mitmproxy} (Python async proxy / TUI),
\texttt{fish-shell} (Rust systems shell), and
\texttt{brew} (Ruby package-manager DSL).
Latest harvest results in \S\ref{subsec:results} report K-side scores for these
suites; paired control (N) runs are pending where marked.

\subsection{Protocol: paired K vs N evaluation}

Each evaluation executes, in order:
(1)~clone and index the suite repository (\texttt{kb init} on first use, or reuse
the named session and \texttt{kb scan} on subsequent runs; \texttt{--force-init}
deletes the base and re-runs \texttt{kb init} from scratch);
(2)~$N_{\text{q}}$ \texttt{kb query} calls (condition \textbf{K})---one-shot synthesis
over the curator-kept fact pool (\S\ref{subsec:synthesis});
(3)~the same questions handed to a real coding agent with no KB
(condition \textbf{N}), when collected;
(4)~LLM scoring ($\times 3$ averaged) on five rubric axes for both sides.
Question counts vary by suite (e.g.\ $N_{\text{q}} = 12$ for \texttt{kb};
expanded suites use $N_{\text{q}} = 8$ with multi-step process/UI-inference items).

Both sides are scored under identical conditions when N is present; the headline
grade is $\Delta S = S_K - S_N$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:delta-s}).

\subsection{Scoring Rubric}

Each suite covers questions spanning capabilities, architecture, installation,
configuration, platform specifics, conventions, gotchas, and (for \texttt{kb})
adversarial or out-of-scope probes---topics that
stress different retrieval paths through the fact graph.  Answers are scored on
five axes:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{4.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Axis} & \textbf{Criterion} \\
\midrule
Correctness      & Factually grounded in the repo/KB \\
Usefulness       & Helps a developer act or understand \\
Relevance        & Stays on the question; free of unrelated facts \\
Specificity      & Uses concrete repo-specific details \\
Evidence Handling & Constrained to evidence; acknowledges uncertainty \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Ordinal labels, not bare numbers.}  Rather than ask the LLM
judge for ``an integer 0--4''---which invites guessing a point on an unanchored
scale---each axis is scored by selecting one of five \emph{named ordinal labels}.
Correctness, for example, ranges over
\textit{correct}~(4), \textit{mostly\_correct}~(3), \textit{mixed}~(2),
\textit{mostly\_wrong}~(1), and \textit{no\_answer}~(0); the other axes use
analogous per-axis vocabularies (e.g.\ relevance:
\textit{focused}/\textit{on\_topic}/\textit{padded}/\textit{off\_topic}/\textit{unrelated}).
This reframes scoring as classification---``which description fits this answer?''---rather
than magnitude estimation, which is markedly more reliable for LLM judges.  Each
label maps deterministically to its ordinal level $s \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$, so the
adequacy utility (Eq.~\ref{eq:adequacy}) and every downstream metric operate on the
same $0$--$4$ scale as before and historical runs remain comparable. Deterministic
indicators (token usage, latency) are measured numerically and are never labeled.

\subsection{Success Score and Headline Verdict ($\Delta S$)}
\label{subsec:success}

The paired harvest evaluation's single scalar per side is $S \in [0,1]$ (higher is better).
It trades \emph{adequate} answer quality against operational cost: we care that
responses are correct and meaningful, but rubric scores above an acceptability
threshold $\tau$ yield diminishing marginal value---polishing a 3.5 into a 4.0
should not dominate token or latency savings.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Adequacy utility.}  Each quality axis $s \in [0,4]$ (correctness,
usefulness, relevance) maps to a normalized utility with threshold $\tau = 3$ and excellence
discount $\beta = 0.2$:

\begin{equation}
\varphi_\tau(s) = \frac{1}{1+\beta}\left(
  \min\!\left(1,\;\frac{s}{\tau}\right)
  + \beta \cdot \frac{\max(0,\; s - \tau)}{4 - \tau}
\right)
\label{eq:adequacy}
\end{equation}

\noindent Below $\tau$, quality rises linearly (inadequate answers are heavily
penalized).  At $s = \tau$, $\varphi_\tau = 1/(1+\beta) \approx 0.83$; at $s = 4$,
$\varphi_\tau = 1$.  The gap from ``acceptable'' to ``perfect'' is therefore
$\approx 17\%$ of the quality scale---not the $33\%$ implied by linear
$(c+u)/8$ scoring.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Composite score.}  The harvest success score blends adequacy
quality with token economy and speed:

\begin{equation}
S = w_Q \cdot Q_{\text{adeq}} + w_T \cdot E_{\text{tok}} + w_V \cdot E_{\text{speed}}
\label{eq:success}
\end{equation}

\noindent with default weights $w_Q = 0.60$, $w_T = 0.30$, $w_V = 0.10$:

\begin{align}
Q_{\text{adeq}} &= \tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(\varphi_\tau(\bar{c}) + \varphi_\tau(\bar{u}) + \varphi_\tau(\bar{r})\bigr), \notag \\
E_{\text{tok}} &= 1 - \min\!\Bigl(\tfrac{T}{B_T},\,1\Bigr), \notag \\
E_{\text{speed}} &= 1 - \min\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\tau_w}{B_\tau},\,1\Bigr)
\label{eq:success-components}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\bar{c}, \bar{u}, \bar{r}$ are mean correctness, usefulness, and relevance ($0$--$4$),
so an answer that drags in unrelated facts is penalized even when correct and useful,
$T$ is the weighted token total for the question side
($\text{input} + \text{output} + 0.1 \times \text{cache\_read}$ for control agents;
kb query uses undifferentiated input$+$output), and $\tau_w$ is
wall-clock latency.  Budgets $B_T = 10^6$ tokens and $B_\tau = 6\times 10^5$\,ms
are fixed (budget-absolute).  KB (K) and control (N) use the identical formula.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Headline project grade.}  The single number that answers
\emph{does KB beat a real coding agent?} is the success-score \emph{difference}
in the same evaluation run:

\begin{equation}
\Delta S = S_K - S_N
\label{eq:delta-s}
\end{equation}

\noindent $\Delta S \geq +0.02$ $\Rightarrow$ KB \textbf{ahead of control};
$\Delta S \leq -0.02$ $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{behind}; otherwise \textbf{on par}.
Per-side scores $S_K$ and $S_N$ come from Eq.~\ref{eq:success}; their component
parts ($Q_{\text{adeq}}$, $E_{\text{tok}}$, $E_{\text{speed}}$) show \emph{where} KB wins or
loses (e.g.\ adequate quality at lower exploration cost).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Secondary diagnostics.}  Pass rate and per-axis rubric means
(correctness, usefulness, relevance, etc.) help explain a $\Delta S$ outcome but are
not the headline grade.  We also report the fraction of questions where
correctness, usefulness, \emph{and} relevance all score $\geq 3$ on the 0--4 scale.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Retrieval-side relevance.}  For condition~K, we log how many
retrieved facts the curator kept, dropped, and re-queried (\S\ref{subsec:curation}),
and compute retrieval precision as $\text{kept}/(\text{kept}+\text{dropped})$.
This complements answer-level relevance in the rubric: low precision with high
answer relevance indicates heavy pre-synthesis filtering; low answer relevance
despite curation signals synthesis or gap-fill gaps.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Scoring stability.}  LLM-as-judge scoring is
non-deterministic even at temperature~0; we average three scorer calls per question.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Quality vs.\ efficiency trade-off.}  On the paired suites the control
agent leads on adequacy ($Q_{\text{adeq}} = 1.0$) and mean rubric scores
(correctness/usefulness/relevance at or near 4.0), while K trails slightly on
answer quality (e.g.\ kb self-check correctness \KbSelfCheckKCorrectness{},
raylib \RaylibKCorrectness{}).  K nonetheless wins $\Delta S$ because the success
score weights token economy and latency heavily ($w_T{=}0.30$, $w_V{=}0.10$):
K's weighted token totals are an order of magnitude smaller than N's on both
suites, and K completes the query phase faster on raylib.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Retrieval cost profile.}  Per-question telemetry from these runs
shows the deep BFS loop and curator dominate K-side cost: thinking (retrieval +
in-loop judges) accounts for $\approx$72--73\% of query tokens vs.\ $\approx$27--28\%
for final synthesis; the curator drops $\approx$97\% of facts that reach it before
synthesis (typical kept counts in the single digits to low teens).  On kb
self-check, 6/12 questions and on raylib 5/8 questions ran the loop to exhaustion
(mean $\approx$7.8 and 10.8 passes per question respectively) rather than exiting
early on the sufficiency judge---so $\Delta S$ gains coexist with a retrieval loop
that still spends most of its budget exploring rather than synthesizing.
